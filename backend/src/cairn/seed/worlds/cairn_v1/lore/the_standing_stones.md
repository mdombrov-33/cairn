Long before the Border Wars, before any crown drew a line across the Reach, someone raised
standing stones along the high ground of the north country — grey monoliths in rings and rows,
worn faceless by weather, older than any story that survives to explain them. The Reach-born
give them a wide berth and a short prayer. The heartland scholars who came to measure them left
early and wrote little.

What the country folk agree on, in the careful way people speak of things they'd rather not, is
that the stones mark **deep places** — old hollows and shafts and barrow-mouths where the ground
does not feel finished, where a light will gutter for no wind and a road will run a mile longer
going than coming back. Nothing happened at the stones for generations; the Quiet Peace lay over
them like it lay over everything. But peace is only the absence of attention, and lately the
attention of something has turned. On the Mill Road, which runs up past the oldest ring of all,
travelers have begun not to arrive. The old men at the watchforts have stopped pretending they
don't hear it, out there, in the nights with no moon.
