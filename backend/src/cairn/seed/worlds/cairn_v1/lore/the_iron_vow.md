## Origin

The Iron Vow began as a single company of the Border Wars, raised in the second decade of the
fighting when the heartland powers had already begun to husband their own regulars and lean on
hired steel for the ugly work along the frontier. Most free companies of that era were little
better than licensed brigands — paid to fight, paid better to stop, and gone the moment a purse
ran dry. The Vow was the exception, and it was the exception on purpose. Its founder, a
disgraced heartland officer whose name the order no longer speaks, built it around a single
idea that the mercenary trade had forgotten: that a company whose word could be *trusted* — to
finish a contract, to hold a line, to keep faith with an employer and with each other — would
outlast and out-earn any band of cutthroats. They took a banner of three black crows on a grey
field, carrion birds who come only after the killing is done, and they made the joke their
creed: *we are what's left when the fighting's over.*

## The creed

The Vow's discipline was legendary and its rules were few, but the few were absolute. A contract
taken is a contract finished. Faith is kept with your own before any employer, any coin, any
crown. And the debt is always paid — the one you owe and the one owed to you, in full, however
long it takes. Recruits did not sign papers; they *took the crow*, a small sigil pricked into the
inside of the left forearm, and the taking was understood to be for life. There was no honorable
discharge from the Iron Vow, only service, death, or — the word they spit — desertion. Among a
company that kept faith above all, to take the crow and then walk was the single unforgivable
thing, worse than any honest enemy, and the Vow has never in its history let such a debt go
unsettled.

## The scattering

No treaty ended the Border Wars; the powers simply ran out of coin and bodies and withdrew to
their heartlands, and the Iron Vow, like every company on the frontier, was left unpaid and
unneeded in the same season. Most such companies dissolved into the trades — a smith here, a
caravan guard there, a farmer who no longer flinches at thunder. Many Vow veterans did the same,
hanging up the crow and speaking of certain campaigns to no one. But the order did not die the way
the others did, because the order was never only a paycheck. Under the last commander to hold it
together, the Vow turned its discipline to the one thing the abandoned Reach still needed and no
one else would honor: the keeping of promises in a country with no law left to keep them.

## What they became

Three generations into the Quiet Peace, the Iron Vow is no longer an army — it may be no more
than a few dozen sworn and a wider net of veterans who still answer the three crows. But it is a
*power*, in the particular way that a thing everyone fears is a power. Where no court sits and no
lord will ride, the Vow does the work the law abandoned: protection that actually lasts, the
recovery of what was stolen, the escort of what must not be lost — and, above all, the settlement
of debts that no magistrate would hear. A Vow contract is spoken of the way people speak of a
verdict. You do not cross one twice. The three crows do not threaten and do not haggle; they
state the terms, and then they are patient, and the patience is the frightening part.

## Recognizing them

A Vow veteran is known by the small crow sigil inside the left forearm, and known better by what
they will not do: they do not boast of the Wars, do not name the campaigns they fought, and do not
break a given word even when breaking it would be easy and wise. Some wear the mark openly. Most
keep it under a sleeve — because bounty-hunters and old enemies know it too, and because a mark
that means *my word is iron* also means *I have killed for this.* Ask a veteran about the crow and
you will get silence, a change of subject, or, if you have earned it, a single true sentence and
nothing more.

## The unspoken truth

The Vow is far smaller than the fear of it, and the fear is the last of its capital. The order
spends enormous effort keeping that difference invisible, because the day the Reach learns how few
crows are truly left is the day the debts stop being paid — and the debts are the only thing the
Wars did not burn. Everyone in the Reach has heard of the Iron Vow. Almost no one has met more
than one of them. Both of those facts are, very deliberately, kept true.
